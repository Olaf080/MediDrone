# MediDrone - Making defibrillators fly

About 275,000 Europeans have an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest each year, the American statistics prove even more concerning at 350,000. At best, 10% of them survive to hospital discharge. What is the bottleneck ? During cardiac arrest, the heart suddenly stops pumping blood, cutting off oxygen and nutrients to the brain and vital organs. Tissue damage begins within minutes and worsens rapidly the longer blood flow stays stopped. Furthermore, the only viable treatment a bystander can give is CPR, that is, if they do not have an AED (automated external defibrillator) at hand. 

A 2025 study by the Cardiac Arrest Registry to Enhance Survival (CARES) investigated out-of-hospital cardiac arrests : 13.9 % of patients who arrested in public had a bystander applied AED, 71.4 % of arrests happen in a residential setting (17.5 % in public and 11.1 % in nursing homes) and finally the median EMS response time was 7.6 min. The dots aren't hard to connect, reducing cardiac arrest fatality can be addressed by cutting down the time to response and providing resources to apply AED's. Ambulances, whilst being the highest-quality medical aid out there, are limited by speed. In the city, traffic is the main concern, outside of it, distances and spotty coverages are the new perpetrators.

We think high-speed drones are the golden ticket for this problem. The record for the fastest groundspeed by a quadcopter was recently broken in December 2025, now standing at an impressive 657.59 km/h. To put that into perspective, the drone could cross London in under 5 minutes. A feat that takes closer to an hour by car. Though that speed mightn't be realistic for our medical drone, it serves to prove a point. There is a clear inefficiency when it comes to emergency medical response. A challenge the MediDrone team is ecstatic to take on
